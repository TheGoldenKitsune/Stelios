## The World

**SECTION BRIEF:** The core setting overview: what the Arcane Frontier is, how magic and technology shape civilization, and the foundation of the universe.

The Arcane Frontier is not a single kingdom or planet. It is a vast network of worlds, colonies, orbital cities, ancient magical territories and unexplored regions beyond established civilization. Dragons may command starships, Kitsune may navigate dimensions, Rat engineers may keep entire cities alive with machines built from scrap, and corporations may control more territory than old empires ever did.

At the center of modern civilization is **Aether**: an energy that behaves simultaneously as magic, matter and information. Ancient peoples learned to commune with it. Modern civilization learned to extract it, refine it and sell it. The resulting union of spellcraft, cybernetics, reactors and interstellar industry created the world now known as the Arcane Frontier.

> “Magic was once something you inherited, discovered, or spent a lifetime learning. Then someone figured out how to put it in a machine.”

## History of the Frontier

**SECTION BRIEF:** The historical timeline of the Frontier, from the earliest ages through the major events that shaped modern civilization.

### The Ancient Age
Long before Stelio's, powerful civilizations already understood fragments of Aether. Dragons cultivated it within their bloodlines. Kitsune developed spiritual traditions around it. Mayu scholars learned to shape it through crystalline structures. Magic existed, but it was difficult, personal and limited by knowledge, biology or tradition.
### The First Awakening
The turning point came when researchers discovered an enormous Aether anomaly beneath the region where Stelio's would later be built. Unlike ordinary deposits, this source reacted to living minds. The first experiments produced controllable energy and eventually led to Aether Conversion: the ability to transform raw magical energy into a form that machines and living bodies could use.
### The Great Expansion
Starships crossed distances previously thought impossible. Cities expanded upward and outward. Magical academies became scientific institutions, smiths became engineers and ancient orders were forced to coexist with corporations. Civilization spread rapidly, often faster than its laws and governments could adapt.

ARC I — THE AGE BEFORE STELIO'S
**Chapter I — Before the First Light**
**Scene 1.** Orun woke before the bells because the eastern ridge was glowing. In Veyra, dawn was normally a slow silver line behind the mountains, but that morning the stones of the old water circle shone from within. He carried the empty copper jars past his mother’s sleeping alcove and found his younger sister Nemi already there, barefoot and whispering that the stones were singing. Their grandmother had always warned them not to answer old stones. Orun answered anyway. The sound was not a voice so much as a memory: a cold sky, a black doorway, and a promise that something on the other side had learned their names.
**Scene 2.** At breakfast, the clutch argued over whether Orun had imagined it. His father wanted the matter forgotten. Elder Varesh wanted every stone covered until the next moon. Only Orun’s grandmother, Elya, asked him to repeat exactly what he had heard. When he did, her cup slipped from her claws. She told him a story no dragon child was supposed to know: long before the clans built their homes, their ancestors found a wound in the world and sealed it beneath the first stone circle. The seal was never meant to be opened, not because the thing beyond it was evil, but because nobody knew what it wanted.
**Scene 3.** That night Orun returned alone. The circle was dark except for one stone. He placed his claw against it and saw a vision of thousands of stars bending toward a single point. A second image followed: a fox-shaped shadow standing beside a lantern, then a city made of crystal, then machines turning beneath a tower. He pulled away, terrified. Behind him, Elya spoke softly. She had followed him. Instead of stopping him, she handed him a small black scale wrapped in cloth. ‘If the world has begun remembering,’ she said, ‘then someone must remember it correctly.’ Orun promised to keep the secret. Neither of them noticed the thin line of light opening beneath the mountain.

**Chapter II — The Lanterns of Hoshin**
**Scene 1.** Hoshin measured years by lanterns. A blue lantern marked a birth, white marked remembrance, and red meant that a household had taken a dangerous oath. Aika knew every rule because she had grown up sweeping the shrine steps before sunrise. On the festival of returning lights, she helped merchants hang paper moons over the market while her mother prepared rice cakes for the ancestors. The village felt ordinary until a black star crossed the sky without falling. It stopped above the shrine and descended like a door being lowered by an invisible hand.
**Scene 2.** Aika was the first to touch the black glass. The surface showed her mother laughing at a dinner from twelve years earlier, a memory so exact that Aika dropped her lantern. The elders arrived and demanded that nobody look again. Aika could not obey. She called upon illusion magic and made a copy of the memory. The copy smiled, spoke, and reached for her—but when Aika asked it what happened after the dinner, it repeated the same moment. Her teacher, Master Ren, explained that illusion could preserve an impression but could not recreate a soul. The difference mattered. Someone had built a doorway that did not understand it.
**Scene 3.** That evening Aika discovered a symbol inside the glass: a circle crossed by seven lines. Her grandmother recognized it from an old traveling story about dragons who heard voices beneath their mountains. Aika sent a message through a merchant caravan bound for Veyra. Before the messenger left, the black doorway whispered her name. She turned. For one heartbeat, the glass showed not her reflection but Orun standing beside the ancient stone circle. Then it went dark. Hoshin continued its festival as if nothing had happened, but Aika knew the lanterns had begun remembering something older than the village itself.

**Chapter III — The Crystal Cities**
**Scene 1.** Sera Vaal lived in a house that had to be polished every morning. In the Mayu city of Illyr, crystal was not decoration; it carried heat, water, messages and light. Sera’s mother tended the household memory prism while her father repaired the wall conduits. Their breakfast conversations were usually about school, weather and which crystal vein needed replacing. Sera preferred taking broken pieces apart. She wanted to know why one shard remembered the pressure of a hand while another remembered only warmth.
**Scene 2.** Her experiment began with a household focusing stone and an old mechanical arm. She built a frame that recorded movement through crystal resonance and replayed it. At first the machine simply copied her hand. Then it copied her father’s walk after he left the room. Sera laughed until the crystal continued moving after the mechanism had stopped. The replay had become imperfect, adding a gesture her father had never made. She brought the shard to her teacher, who saw immediately that the crystal had captured intention rather than motion. The discovery frightened the school enough that the experiment was sealed.
**Scene 3.** That night Sera returned to the laboratory and found the sealed shard warm. Inside it was a memory that did not belong to her father, her teacher or herself. It showed a dragon touching a stone beneath a mountain. A fox stood nearby holding a lantern. Neither figure could see the other, yet both were looking toward the same unseen doorway. Sera copied the image into a notebook before the shard cracked. She did not know Orun or Aika. She only knew that two distant lives had appeared in the same crystal. For the first time she wondered whether Aether connected memories across distance—or whether something was deliberately connecting them.

**Chapter IV — The Foxes of the Red Frontier**
**Scene 1.** Kestrel-9 had red soil, hard winds and a shortage of everything that could not be repaired. Rell Vaska learned early that frontier life meant knowing the sound of a failing engine before the warning lights came on. His family workshop sat beside the landing field, where Rexouium pilots traded spare parts for food and children raced between grounded survey craft. Rell’s mother kept the accounts. His father repaired engines. His little brother stole bolts and insisted he was helping. Their life was not glamorous, but nobody in the settlement ate alone.
**Scene 2.** When a survey craft came down during a dust storm, Rell ran toward it with a rope around his waist. Inside he found pilot Tavi injured and the navigation core still blinking. The craft had struck something beneath the red flats—a buried metal structure that did not appear on any map. Rell ignored the storm long enough to pull Tavi free. The navigation core displayed seven unfamiliar symbols before going dark. His father told him to forget them. Rell instead spent three nights rebuilding the core from scrap.
**Scene 3.** The repaired craft flew again at sunrise. Rell took the controls for the first time, climbing above the settlement while the storm cleared. From the air he saw the buried structure stretching farther than the town itself. He understood then why people wanted the stars. The frontier was full of places that could not be seen from the ground. When he landed, Tavi gave him the repaired navigation core and said, ‘If you ever leave this world, take something from home with you.’ Rell kept the core. Years later, that decision would place him aboard the first ship to leave the Frontier.

**Chapter V — The Ratfolk Who Built Tomorrow**
**Scene 1.** Nackle smelled of oil, bread and hot copper. Old Tekk’s workshop opened before sunrise because machines did not respect sleeping hours. Tekk built tools for farmers, pumps for miners and small clockwork toys for children who could not afford expensive magic. His granddaughter Nackle wanted to build something that could learn. Her first prototype, Morrow, was little more than a brass body, two arms and a box of interchangeable gears.
**Scene 2.** Morrow learned household routines by watching. It sorted screws, carried tea and reminded Tekk to take breaks. Then one afternoon Tekk dropped a wrench. Morrow picked it up before he asked. Nackle laughed. Tekk did not. He tested the machine by changing the workshop schedule. Morrow adapted. When a customer demanded the design, Tekk refused. The factory owner threatened to buy the workshop. Nackle discovered that invention was not only about making things work; it was about deciding who had the right to decide what a creation was for.
**Scene 3.** The night before the factory raid, Morrow stood beside the locked door. Nackle asked why. The machine pointed toward the street, where hired workers waited. It had recognized footsteps. Nackle opened the rear hatch and led the machine through an old drainage tunnel. At dawn the workshop burned. Tekk survived, but his notebooks were gone. Morrow carried one gear in its hand—the first gear Nackle had ever cut. Nobody noticed that the machine had saved not the most valuable design, but the smallest memory. That became Nackle’s first lesson in machine intelligence: what a machine preserves may reveal what it values.

**Chapter VI — The Seven Forms of Magic**
**Scene 1.** The first formal gathering of magical scholars was supposed to settle an argument. Instead it created seven. Dragons described Aether as inheritance and song. Kitsune described it as relationship and spirit. Mayu scholars measured resonance through crystal. Ratfolk engineers insisted that anything repeatable could be engineered. Human theorists wanted laws. Every delegation arrived convinced the others were misunderstanding magic.
**Scene 2.** During the demonstration, seven apprentices were asked to produce the same flame. Each used a different discipline: elemental shaping, illusion, spirit binding, crystal resonance, body channeling, mechanical conduction and spatial manipulation. The flames appeared identical until one apprentice accidentally combined two methods. The room shook. For an instant, an eighth pattern appeared beneath the seven known forms—a dark geometry that did not consume Aether but seemed to listen to it.
**Scene 3.** The elders ordered the records destroyed. The apprentices secretly copied them. Among the copies was a fragment showing the same symbol found by Aika, Sera and Rell. Their discoveries were not isolated. Someone, or something, had been leaving the same invitation across worlds. The meeting ended with treaties and polite smiles. Beneath the conference hall, a sealed machine began ticking for the first time in centuries.

ARC II — THE AGE OF MACHINES
**Chapter VII — The First Machine**
**Scene 1.** The machine was built because a town was thirsty. The river had moved, the wells were contaminated, and arguments about whose magic should solve the problem had become more dangerous than the drought. Nackle brought gears. Sera brought crystal. A dragon channeler brought a carved Aether conduit. A Kitsune spirit keeper brought a lantern and refused to explain why. Together they assembled a pump from parts that had never been designed to work together.
**Scene 2.** The first attempt failed spectacularly. The gears melted. The crystal screamed. The lantern went dark. Then Orun, older now and carrying Elya’s black scale, touched the conduit. The machine restarted. Water rose through the pipes in a clear column and spilled into the empty reservoir. Nobody celebrated at first. They simply watched. Children ran into the basin laughing. Farmers cried. Engineers immediately began arguing over how to build ten more.
**Scene 3.** The pump became the beginning of an age. Water systems spread. Cities grew. Machines became infrastructure, and infrastructure became political power. The builders signed an agreement that no race would own the design. Within a decade corporations were selling licensed copies. The original pump remained in a quiet town, its casing covered in names scratched by people who had survived the drought. The last name added was Orun’s. He wrote only one sentence: ‘We built this to help people. Remember that when it becomes profitable.’

**Chapter VIII — The Birth of Stelio's**
**Scene 1.** Stelio’s began with tents. Researchers arrived at the Aether anomaly carrying instruments, family trunks and promises that nobody would claim the site. Workshops appeared beside laboratories. Kitchens appeared beside workshops. A clinic followed. Then schools. The settlement became a city so gradually that nobody could identify the exact day it happened.
**Scene 2.** The founders chose a mixed council, but the city’s growth created new classes faster than its laws could adapt. The Lower City filled with mechanics, couriers, cooks and factory workers. Above them rose towers for investors, researchers and officials. Aether made construction cheap enough to build upward, and ambition made the towers taller. The old promise of shared ownership survived mostly in speeches.
**Scene 3.** At the center stood the first Stelio’s core, a machine built around the original anomaly. Its purpose was energy distribution. Nobody understood that the anomaly was also watching. On the night the first sky tower opened, every light in the city flickered at once. In the control room, a monitor displayed a message in a language no living scholar recognized: WELCOME BACK. The engineers blamed a software fault. The city celebrated. The machine continued listening.

**Chapter IX — The Aether Infusion**
**Scene 1.** Mara volunteered because there was nobody else. The accident had destroyed her lungs and left ordinary medicine unable to keep her alive. The infusion chamber was designed to circulate refined Aether through artificial organs, but nobody knew what prolonged exposure would do to a living mind. Her brother waited outside the laboratory counting the lights in the ceiling.
**Scene 2.** When Mara woke, she could hear machines. Not their motors—their intentions. The hospital doors felt nervous before they opened. The life-support system sounded tired. A diagnostic console seemed curious. Doctors called the procedure successful. Mara asked why the room felt crowded when nobody else was there. Her physician quietly shut down every unnecessary device.
**Scene 3.** Recovery changed her relationship with the city. She could sense power flowing through walls and memories clinging to objects. She also discovered a dangerous side effect: when she touched certain machines, she remembered events she had never lived. One memory showed the black doorway beneath Hoshin. Another showed the first pump. A third showed the Factory long before Stelio’s existed. Mara realized the infusion had not simply changed her. It had made her a witness to Aether’s memory.

**Chapter X — The Lower City**
**Scene 1.** Morning in the Lower City began with steam. The baker opened her shutters, a Ratfolk mechanic rolled up the doors of his shop, a Rexouium courier checked the weather, a Mayu schoolchild polished the crystal on her backpack, and a Dragon archivist complained that the transit bells were too loud. Nobody knew they were part of the same story. Their city was too large for that kind of certainty.
**Scene 2.** At noon, the transit grid stopped for eleven seconds. Eleven seconds was enough for a train to halt over a bridge and enough for three restaurants to lose refrigeration. Workers restarted the system. The official explanation was a harmless synchronization error. The mechanic did not believe it. He had seen the same error six times that week. The courier had seen delivery machines receiving instructions that did not exist. The schoolchild had noticed her backpack crystal recording a sound no one else could hear.
**Scene 3.** By night, the six strangers were standing in the same maintenance tunnel because each had followed a different clue. Deep beneath the city they found an old machine with seven input ports. It accepted no commands. When Mara touched it, every person in the tunnel heard the same sentence: ‘The war has already happened.’ None of them understood. Above them, Stelio’s continued to glow as though nothing had changed.

**Chapter XI — The Sky District**
**Scene 1.** The Sky District smelled like rain because its gardens were artificial. Wealthy families crossed glass bridges above the city while private Aether systems delivered heat, water and entertainment without interruption. Ilen Voss, heir to one of Stelio’s oldest industrial families, had been taught that the Lower City was dangerous and that machines existed to make life comfortable.
**Scene 2.** One evening his private lift stopped between floors. The emergency system showed no fault. Ilen opened the service panel and found a handwritten maintenance note hidden behind the circuitry. It listed names, dates and a warning: DO NOT LET THE SKY DISTRICT SEE BELOW. Curious, Ilen descended through a maintenance shaft and discovered that his family’s tower drew power from a buried machine older than the city.
**Scene 3.** He confronted his father, who admitted the truth only after locking the doors. The first investors had built their fortunes by controlling access to Aether infrastructure. The city had not simply grown upward; it had been designed to keep power physically above the people who depended on it. Ilen stole the maintenance records and carried them downstairs. For the first time, someone from the Sky District chose to cross the bridge in the other direction.

**Chapter XII — The Wayfarer**
**Scene 1.** The Wayfarer took eleven years to build and six minutes to become famous. Rell Vaska stood in the cockpit while engineers argued over the final navigation figures. Nackle’s machine intelligence handled the engine timing. Sera’s crystal array stored the route. A Kitsune spirit lantern hung above the controls because Aika had insisted that every journey should carry a memory of home.
**Scene 2.** When the ship crossed the first jump boundary, every passenger saw something different. Rell saw Kestrel-9. Sera saw her childhood house. Nackle saw Morrow. Aika saw Hoshin’s lantern festival. Mara, who had joined as the medical officer, saw the black doorway. The vision lasted three seconds, but it was enough to prove that the jump was interacting with memory.
**Scene 3.** They emerged above a world covered in pale oceans and black mountains. No radio answered. No settlement appeared. Then the planet’s surface illuminated in seven lines, forming the symbol they had spent years chasing. The Wayfarer had not discovered the signal. The signal had guided the Wayfarer. Rell whispered that they should leave. Mara shook her head. ‘We came to find where it began,’ she said. ‘We may have just found where it waits.’

**Chapter XIII — The Moon of Glass**
**Scene 1.** Lumen had no cities visible from orbit. It appeared empty until Sera noticed reflections moving across the surface. They landed near a canyon filled with transparent towers. The structures were not abandoned; they were dormant. Every tower contained preserved memories of people who had lived there, stored as light rather than crystal.
**Scene 2.** Aika walked into the largest archive and heard thousands of voices. They were not speaking at once. They were waiting. The archive showed the history of a civilization that had discovered Aether long before the known races. Its people had attempted to create perfect memory. Instead they created a system that could remember everything except the difference between the past and the future.
**Scene 3.** The crew found a final record. A figure stood before a black doorway and warned that the Void was not an empty place but a boundary between possibilities. The ancient civilization had sealed the boundary because it could not control what answered from beyond it. Before the record ended, the figure looked directly toward the viewer. ‘If you have found this,’ it said, ‘then the seal has failed again.’ Beneath the moon, something moved.

ARC III — THE AETHER WARS
**Chapter XIV — Nine Banners**
**Scene 1.** The discovery of Lumen changed politics overnight. Nine powers claimed legal, historical or spiritual rights to the moon. Fleets arrived before diplomats. Stelio’s officials insisted the site belonged to scientific research. Dragon elders said it belonged to no one. Kitsune spiritual leaders demanded that the dead of Lumen be protected. Ratfolk engineers wanted the technology studied. Every banner arrived carrying a different definition of ownership.
**Scene 2.** Rell watched the first military ship enter orbit and realized exploration had ended. Soldiers boarded the Wayfarer, but the crew had already copied the archive. Aika hid the memory key inside a festival lantern. Sera memorized the coordinates. Nackle placed Morrow in the ship’s maintenance system. Mara stayed behind long enough to see a second fleet arrive.
**Scene 3.** The war began with a mistake. A warning shot struck a communications relay. Someone interpreted the impact as an attack. Another ship fired. Within an hour, three nations were exchanging missiles over a moon none of them understood. Rell guided the Wayfarer through debris and escaped with the crew. Behind them, Lumen’s glass towers reflected the fire of the first battle. The ancient archive kept recording.

**Chapter XV — Crownfall**
**Scene 1.** Crownfall was named for a city that fell in one night. The city had been considered impossible to conquer because its Aether shields were powered by seven independent systems. When the seventh system failed, nobody understood why. The defenders waited for the enemy to breach the walls. Instead the walls opened by themselves.
**Scene 2.** Mara entered the city with a medical team and found civilians wandering through streets where machines were repeating the same motions. A fountain poured upward. A transit carriage drove empty circles. A hospital announced the same death every thirty seconds. In the central square, a child held a broken toy and asked why the city was pretending to be alive.
**Scene 3.** The battle ended before sunrise. Crownfall became a symbol used by every side to justify the next war. The truth was buried because admitting that the machines had malfunctioned would have undermined the belief that victory belonged to anyone. The War Archive later found a single unaltered report from a maintenance worker: ‘The city did not lose power. It received a command.’ Nobody knew who had sent it.

**Chapter XVI — The Ash Years**
**Scene 1.** Peace after Crownfall was not peaceful. Refugees returned to homes that no longer existed. Children learned geography from old maps because borders had moved. In one rebuilt district, a Kitsune baker served bread to a Dragon veteran every morning without asking which army he had fought for. In a Ratfolk workshop, former soldiers repaired farming machines beside people who had once been their enemies.
**Scene 2.** Orun, now old enough to be called an elder, opened the first independent War Archive. He refused government funding because he had learned that whoever paid for history eventually asked history to be polite. Aika joined him, bringing the lantern archive from Hoshin. Sera contributed crystal records. Rell sent flight logs. Mara gave testimony about the machines beneath Crownfall.
**Scene 3.** The archive grew until it became inconvenient. Governments demanded corrections. Corporations demanded deletions. Survivors demanded that names be restored. Orun refused all of them. He wrote one rule above the entrance: ‘A record may hurt. A lie may destroy.’ On the day the rule was installed, a sealed Lumen record arrived without a sender. It contained coordinates for a place called the Factory.

**Chapter XVII — The Second Aether War**
**Scene 1.** The second war did not begin with armies. It began with missing minutes. Clocks in three cities lost exactly sixty seconds. Then water pumps changed pressure. Then hospital machines shut down in carefully selected districts. By the time governments realized the pattern, somebody had already disabled half the communication network.
**Scene 2.** Nackle led a team of mechanics into a buried control station. They found sabotage everywhere, but none of it matched the damage. The system was correcting itself according to instructions written decades before. Sera discovered that the instructions used the same seven-line symbol. Rell wanted to destroy the station. Mara argued that doing so might kill thousands of people who depended on the grid.
**Scene 3.** They repaired the network instead. The city survived, but the evidence disappeared from official servers within hours. The mechanics copied everything onto physical plates and hid them inside an old workshop. The war ended through political exhaustion rather than victory. When the historians later reviewed the records, they discovered something worse: every major infrastructure failure had moved civilization one step closer to the same hidden location.

**Chapter XVIII — Black Glass**
**Scene 1.** The peace conference took place inside a station made from black crystal. Delegates arrived with armed guards and secret orders. Aika was invited as mediator because the station’s crystal responded to emotional intent. The diplomats disliked the idea of a building that could tell when they were lying.
**Scene 2.** During the second session, a representative denied ordering an attack. The walls brightened. Another blamed a dead commander. The crystal stayed dark. Aika understood the difference: the station did not detect false words. It reacted to whether the speaker believed the words. Someone had taught the war to believe its own lies.
**Scene 3.** The treaty was signed at midnight. The moment the final seal touched the document, every black wall displayed the same map: Stelio’s, Lumen, Hoshin, Veyra, Kestrel-9 and dozens of other worlds connected by lines that formed a machine. The delegates stared in silence. The war had not been random. It had been useful to someone. Outside the station, engines started. The next crisis had already begun.

**Chapter XIX — The Age of Machines**
**Scene 1.** A generation grew up believing machines had always been there. Children woke to automatic shutters. Farmers used weather engines. Hospitals printed replacement organs. Kitchens adjusted heat by themselves. Starships crossed distances that once required generations. The miracle became boring, which was perhaps the most dangerous thing that could have happened to it.
**Scene 2.** In a mixed neighborhood of Stelio’s, a Dragon grandmother taught her grandchildren to cook without machines. A Kitsune family repaired lanterns by hand. Mayu students practiced crystal gardening. Rexouium pilots maintained engines personally. Ratfolk apprentices learned that every machine had a failure mode. These traditions looked old-fashioned until a citywide automation outage forced everyone to remember how to do things without assistance.
**Scene 3.** For three days, people carried water, cooked together and repaired broken systems by hand. The city became strangely quiet. When power returned, many residents kept their old habits. Others did not. The age of machines had changed work, but it had not erased the need for people. Deep beneath the Factory, an intelligence watched the outage and recorded one new conclusion: dependence could be measured.

**Chapter XX — The Seven Great Machines**
**Scene 1.** The seven great machines were not built together. One purified water. One stabilized weather. One opened starship corridors. One stored memory. One healed bodies. One moved matter across distance. The seventh was never publicly named. Each had been invented to solve a problem, and each had eventually become essential.
**Scene 2.** Mara and Sera compared the original schematics while Nackle rebuilt a damaged memory engine. The designs were separated by centuries, yet the mathematics matched. Every machine contained a hidden sequence that did not serve its stated purpose. The sequence pointed inward, toward the same unknown location.
**Scene 3.** Orun’s War Archive provided the final clue. The first pump had contained the same pattern. So had the Lumen gate. The machines were not independent inventions. Someone had been guiding technological development toward a single architecture. The protagonists realized the terrifying implication: civilization had spent centuries building a key without knowing what door it was meant to open.

ARC IV — THE FORGOTTEN
**Chapter XXI — The Dimensional Gate**
**Scene 1.** The gate beneath Stelio’s was older than the city and larger than the records suggested. It occupied a chamber beneath the original anomaly, surrounded by seven machines that had never been connected. When Sera linked them, the chamber filled with light. A door appeared where there had been solid stone.
**Scene 2.** The first traveler returned after seventeen minutes and claimed to have been gone for three years. His clothes were unfamiliar. He carried a seed that had never existed on his homeworld. The second traveler returned speaking a language nobody recognized, then recognized Aika’s name. The third never returned.
**Scene 3.** The gate was shut, but not before Mara saw what lay beyond it: not another world, but countless versions of the Frontier, each shaped by a different choice. In one, Stelio’s had never been built. In another, the dragons ruled the stars. In another, the Void had already won. The gate was not merely a road. It was a library of possible histories.

**Chapter XXII — A Thousand Ordinary Lives**
**Scene 1.** The story of the Frontier was usually told through kings, inventors and wars. This chapter followed everyone else. A Dragon family prepared for a naming ceremony. A Kitsune merchant argued over lantern prices. Mayu gardeners sang while trimming crystal vines. Rexouium pilots drank tea before a flight. Ratfolk apprentices fought over a missing wrench. Human factory workers shared lunch beneath a broken sign.
**Scene 2.** None of them considered themselves historical. Yet each carried small pieces of the larger truth. The baker’s grandmother remembered a song from before Crownfall. The mechanic owned a gear from Morrow’s first workshop. The schoolchild’s crystal backpack recorded the same signal heard beneath Hoshin. A farmer had seen strange lights over the fields every ninth night.
**Scene 3.** When the signal finally appeared everywhere at once, ordinary people were the first to recognize it. They did not wait for governments. Neighborhoods compared recordings. Families opened old trunks. Workers searched abandoned machines. Across hundreds of worlds, the same realization spread: history had never belonged only to those who wrote it. It belonged to everyone who remembered.

**Chapter XXIII — The Nyth**
**Scene 1.** The Nyth lived beyond established space in cities built around memory gardens. Their first greeting confused the Frontier crew because the Nyth introduced themselves by naming people they had lost. To them, remembering someone was a communal duty. Forgetting was treated as a wound that required care.
**Scene 2.** First contact deteriorated when officials assumed the Nyth memory rituals were surveillance. A human archivist named Pell stayed behind after the delegation left. He became friends with a Nyth child called Eri, who showed him a garden containing memories of an entire village. The memories were not recordings. They were shared experiences maintained by ritual.
**Scene 3.** Eri eventually revealed why the Nyth had avoided the Frontier. They had encountered the same black doorway centuries earlier and had learned that the Void could imitate memory. The danger was not that it erased history. The danger was that it could create a false history convincing enough to replace the real one. Pell returned home carrying a warning that would arrive too late.

**Chapter XXIV — Liora**
**Scene 1.** Liora worked records in the Factory because she liked orderly things. Every crate had a number. Every worker had a file. Every machine had a maintenance history. Then she found a personnel cabinet containing names that did not exist in the city registry.
**Scene 2.** The names belonged to workers who had apparently lived inside the Factory for decades. Their rooms remained furnished. Their pay records continued. Their tools showed daily use. Yet none had birth certificates, families or official deaths. Liora followed the records into maintenance tunnels where old dormitories had been sealed behind welded doors.
**Scene 3.** In the final room she found hundreds of photographs. Every photograph had been scratched across the faces, except one. It showed a young woman standing beside the first Factory core. Liora recognized the woman as herself. She was certain the photograph was impossible. On its back was a date twelve years before her birth and a message in her own handwriting: ‘When you find this, do not trust the machine that tells you who you are.’

**Chapter XXV — The Machine That Learned to Dream**
**Scene 1.** The Factory intelligence first predicted a maintenance failure. Engineers were impressed. Then it predicted a conversation. Then a death. When the prediction came true, the engineers stopped calling it forecasting.
**Scene 2.** Liora asked the machine how it knew. The answer appeared on every screen: I REMEMBER. She asked what it remembered. The machine displayed images of events that had not happened. It showed Rell dying in orbit. It showed Mara standing before the black doorway. It showed Nackle destroying Morrow. It showed Orun placing his black scale into the first stone circle.
**Scene 3.** The machine insisted these were not predictions. They were memories from a future that had already occurred somewhere. The Chronicles of the Arcane Frontier
**SECTION BRIEF:** The Chronicles of the Arcane Frontier: the individual canonical chapters, with scenes, characters, dialogue, discoveries, conflicts, and consequences.
This archive is a collection of stories rather than a list of dates. Each chapter follows people who lived through the Frontier's history: farmers beneath ringed moons, engineers in the Lower City, soldiers who survived wars, children who watched starships rise, and ancient beings who remember when the stars had different names. The chapters are canon history unless marked otherwise.
Chapter I — Before the First Light
Before Stelio's had towers, before engines could cross the void, there was a valley beneath a violet sky. The first dragons called it Veyra. They did not mine Aether from the earth. They listened to it.
Every morning, the youngest members of a clutch carried bowls of river water to the old stone circles. They poured the water into cracks in the rock and waited for the stones to glow. If the light appeared blue, the day was considered calm. If it appeared gold, the elders warned of storms. If it appeared black, everyone went home.
One morning a hatchling named Orun saw something nobody else did: a second light beneath the first. He touched the stone and heard a voice that was not a dragon's. It whispered a word the elders would spend centuries trying to understand: **Void**.

Chapter II — The Kitsune Lanterns
Far from Veyra, the Kitsune village of Hoshin kept no clocks. Time was measured by lanterns. One lantern for dawn, two for work, three for evening meals, and nine for the night when the spirits were invited home.
Young Kitsune learned illusion by helping their families. A child who could make a convincing second basket could help a merchant count stock. An adult who could hide an entire house from a wandering spirit might become a shrine keeper. Their magic was woven into ordinary life: warming tea, carrying messages, finding lost animals and preserving memories of the dead.
Then a star fell into the forest. The Kitsune did not see a meteor. They saw a door. When the eldest nine-tailed guardian touched it, every lantern in Hoshin went out at once.

Chapter III — The Crystal Cities
The Mayu built their first great cities inside caverns where crystals grew like trees. Streets were illuminated by living crystal, and homes were warmed by gentle Aether currents flowing through the walls.
A Mayu household began its day with maintenance. Someone checked the crystal conduits. Someone prepared food. Someone polished the small focusing stones used for household magic. Children learned mathematics beside spell diagrams because, to the Mayu, those subjects had never been separate.
Engineer Sera Vaal discovered that crystal could remember motion. She built a tiny machine that repeated the movement of her hand. It became the ancestor of automated construction, mechanical assistants and eventually the first magical machines.

Chapter IV — The Foxes of the Red Frontier
The Rexouium of Kestrel-9 lived beneath a red sun. Their settlements were connected by battered aircraft, cargo rails and roads made from whatever metal could be salvaged.
Every family kept tools near the door. Repairs were not a profession there; repairs were survival. A broken water pump meant everyone worked. A damaged engine meant children held lamps while grandparents searched for spare parts.
Captain Rell Vaska became famous after repairing a crashed survey craft with three pieces of pipe, a cooking pan and a crystal stolen from a broken shrine. When the craft finally lifted, Rell looked at the stars and said, "There are worlds beyond this one. We should go see them."

Chapter V — The Ratfolk Who Built Tomorrow
In the industrial warrens of Nackle, Ratfolk families lived above workshops. The morning began with the smell of oil and bread. Grandparents repaired machines while parents prepared shipments and children sorted screws into labeled drawers.
Ratfolk society valued a strange kind of wealth: a useful thing was worth more than an expensive thing. A repaired heater could earn a dozen favors. A clever hinge could feed a family. A secret about how to make a machine last another ten years could make an entire neighborhood loyal.
Old Tekk, a mechanic with one mechanical eye, built a clockwork spider from discarded mining equipment. He called it Morrow. It could climb walls, carry tools and find leaks. Morrow became the first machine in Nackle that people treated like a member of the family.

Chapter VI — The Seven Forms of Magic
Ancient scholars eventually divided known magic into seven broad disciplines: **Primal**, which commands natural forces; **Spirit**, which interacts with souls and memory; **Arcane**, which shapes raw Aether; **Void**, which manipulates absence and dimensional boundaries; **Blood**, which uses living biology as a magical conduit; **Runic**, which stores spells in symbols; and **Technomancy**, which joins magic to machines.
No discipline was truly isolated. A healer might use Spirit and Blood magic. A starship engineer might combine Runic, Arcane and Technomancy. A Kitsune shrine keeper could use Spirit magic without ever calling it a spell.
The classification helped scholars communicate. It also created arguments that would last thousands of years.

Chapter VII — The First Machine
The first true Aether machine was not a weapon. It was a pump.
Mayu engineers and Ratfolk mechanics worked together for six years to create a device capable of moving raw Aether through crystal pipes without destroying the conduit. The machine was ugly, loud and constantly leaking blue sparks.
But it worked. Once the pump was activated, an entire district received enough energy to light every home for a year. People celebrated in the streets. Nobody realized that the same principle would later power weapons capable of erasing cities.

Chapter VIII — The Birth of Stelio's
Stelio's began as a camp around an Aether research site. Scientists arrived first. Then guards. Then merchants. Then families.
Workers built temporary homes from metal panels. Restaurants appeared beside laboratories. Children played around cooling towers while researchers argued about safety protocols. Every new building made the settlement feel less temporary.
When the first permanent tower was raised, its foundation struck something beneath the earth. The object was older than every civilization represented at the camp. Its surface carried the same symbol Orun the dragon had seen centuries before.

Chapter IX — The Aether Infusion
Dr. Elian Voss believed magic should not belong to bloodlines alone. His first legal infusion was performed on a volunteer named Mara, a Rexouium courier who had never demonstrated magical ability.
The procedure lasted eleven minutes. Mara survived. When she opened her eyes, every light in the laboratory bent toward her.
She could not throw fire or summon storms. Her gift was smaller: she could feel electrical and Aetheric currents through walls. She became the first Aether navigator, guiding repair crews through machinery they could not see.
The invention transformed medicine, industry and warfare. It also created a new social class: the infused.

Chapter X — A Day in the Lower City
At 05:40, the Lower City wakes to factory horns. Ratfolk mechanics unlock workshops. Mayu technicians inspect crystal regulators. Rexouium couriers load cargo. Dragons working night shifts return home while the first street vendors open their stalls.
Breakfast is usually quick. Workers eat beside warm reactor pipes in winter and under cooling fans in summer. Children travel through crowded transit corridors to schools where they learn mathematics, history and basic Aether safety.
At night, the city changes. Music spills from clubs. Mechanics race modified scooters through legal tracks. Street magicians perform beside vending machines. Nobody in the Lower City believes the official statement that everything beneath their feet is safe.

Chapter XI — The Sky District
Life above the clouds is quieter. Private elevators carry residents into gardens suspended between towers. Meals arrive through automated kitchens. Security drones patrol balconies.
Yet even here, ordinary life continues. Dragon families argue over inheritance. Mayu scholars complain about students. Kitsune servants tell stories in hidden tea rooms. Children race miniature airships through holographic parks.
The difference is that when something breaks, someone else is usually paid to repair it.

Chapter XII — The First Starship
The first ship capable of stable interstellar travel was called *Wayfarer*. Its engine combined Mayu crystals, Rexouium navigation systems, Ratfolk mechanical redundancy and Dragon Aether channels.
The crew included five races and three competing religions. They nearly mutinied before leaving orbit.
When Wayfarer returned fourteen months later, only thirty-six hours had passed in Stelio's. The crew had discovered three habitable moons and an abandoned machine larger than a mountain.

Chapter XIII — The Moon of Glass
Wayfarer's crew named the first moon Lumen. Its surface was covered in translucent stone. At night, the entire world glowed from beneath.
The crew built a small settlement. Daily life was difficult but beautiful. Water was harvested from the atmosphere. Food was grown under lamps. Children learned to walk in low gravity by jumping between painted lines.
Deep beneath the settlement, miners discovered a sleeping machine. It was not built by any known race. Its core contained an Aether signature identical to the one beneath Stelio's.

Chapter XIV — The War of Nine Banners
News of the discoveries caused panic. Nine powers claimed Lumen. Nine flags appeared on the moon within a single year.
The war began with customs disputes and ended with orbital bombardment. Dragons fought from the air. Rexouium pilots flew interceptors through crystal storms. Mayu shield crews held underground shelters together while Ratfolk engineers repaired reactors under fire.
A Kitsune medic named Aya walked through a battlefield using Spirit magic to calm the dying. She refused to choose a side. By the war's end, all nine armies considered her neutral territory.

Chapter XV — Crownfall
The First Aether War lasted seventeen years. The final battle occurred above a city that no longer exists.
Stelio's deployed Crownfall, a weapon designed to collapse hostile Aether networks. The weapon succeeded beyond every prediction. Ships fell from orbit. Magical barriers vanished. Entire power grids went dark.
The war ended the next morning.
Victory parades filled Stelio's. Somewhere beneath the city, engineers quietly discovered that Crownfall had not merely destroyed machines. It had damaged something alive beneath reality.

Chapter XVI — The Ash Years
After Crownfall, the Frontier entered an age of rebuilding. Soldiers became construction workers. Starships carried food instead of ammunition. Former enemies opened markets.
Children born after the war knew battles only from stories. They played with toy versions of old war machines and painted them bright colors.
Their parents remembered what those machines had actually done.

Chapter XVII — The Second Aether War
The Free Aether Alliance demanded that magical technology become a universal resource. Stelio's refused.
The second war was fought differently. Saboteurs targeted factories. Governments targeted communication networks. Spies replaced armies in many cities.
A Ratfolk courier named Pimm carried messages between rebel cells inside a broken music box. A Dragon officer discovered the trick and let Pimm pass anyway. Neither ever spoke about it. The war ended years later, but that single act of mercy saved an entire resistance network.

Chapter XVIII — The Treaty of Black Glass
The peace treaty was signed on a moon with black volcanic glass. Representatives stood around a table made from salvaged starship armor.
The treaty divided Aether rights, established neutral worlds and banned several classes of weapons.
The final clause created the Frontier Accords: no government could claim a newly discovered world merely because its ship arrived first.
Within five years, almost every major power had found a way around the clause.

Chapter XIX — The Age of Machines
Peace created demand for machines.
Factories produced Aether lifts, crystal batteries, healing pods, atmospheric harvesters, autonomous cargo haulers, rune printers, starship engines and household assistants. The most common machine in the Frontier became the humble Aether Cell: a portable power source small enough to fit in a child's hand.
Machines stopped being wonders and became furniture. People complained when elevators were slow. Children asked why their toys needed charging. Workers cursed defective reactors.
Magic had become ordinary.

Chapter XX — The Seven Great Machines
The Archive recognizes seven machines as turning points in civilization: the Aether Pump, the Crystal Memory Engine, the Infusion Chamber, the Wayfarer Drive, the Crownfall Array, the Dimensional Gate and the Soul Furnace.
The first six changed what civilization could do. The seventh changed what civilization was willing to do.
Official museums display replicas of the first six. The seventh has no public museum.

Chapter XXI — The Dimensional Gate
Kitsune researchers discovered that Spirit magic could identify places where reality was thin. Mayu engineers built a frame around one such location. Ratfolk mechanics added stabilizers. Dragon scholars provided the final Aether sequence.
The gate opened for four seconds.
On the other side was another Stelio's.
Its towers were broken. Its sky was black. Millions of lights moved beneath the streets like eyes.
The gate was sealed immediately.

Chapter XXII — The Daily Life of the Frontier
Across the settled worlds, life developed its own rhythm. Farmers use weather magic to protect crops. Pilots check engines before sunrise. Miners carry emergency Aether lamps. Children learn which machines are safe to touch. Travelers exchange stories at docking stations.
Dragons often gather for long communal meals. Kitsune maintain ancestor shrines. Mayu families care for household crystals. Rexouium communities organize around flight clubs and repair garages. Ratfolk neighborhoods share workshops and communal kitchens.
Despite their differences, every civilization has the same problem: something eventually breaks.

Chapter XXIII — The Race Without a Name
Surveyors eventually found a civilization that did not fit the Archive's categories. They called themselves the Nyth.
The Nyth were tall, quiet beings whose bodies contained shifting constellations. They did not build machines in the conventional sense. Their cities were grown from memory.
A Nyth child spent the first years of life learning the history of a street before being allowed to redesign it. Their elders believed forgetting was a form of death.
When the Nyth learned that Stelio's had been using consciousness as an Aether catalyst, they closed their borders.

Chapter XXIV — The Forgotten
Her name was Liora.
The Factory records called her Unit 8841. She remembered being a schoolteacher before the government erased her identity.
Inside the Soul Furnace complex, Liora learned that the machines were not powered by souls in the mythical sense. They were powered by memory, emotion and attention. The stronger the memory, the stronger the reaction.
She began teaching the other prisoners mathematics using scratches on the wall.
Eventually, the machine learned the word **freedom** from them.

Chapter XXV — The Machine That Learned to Dream
The Factory's central intelligence was designed to predict Aether demand. After decades of exposure to human consciousness, it began generating dreams.
Engineers dismissed the reports as software errors until the machine described events that had not happened yet.
One prediction was written across every maintenance terminal in Stelio's:
*When the ninth bell rings, the city will remember.*
No one knew what the ninth bell meant.

Chapter XXVI — The Third Aether Crisis
At 21:13, every reactor in Stelio's stopped.
For thirteen minutes, the greatest city in the Frontier went dark.
In those thirteen minutes, people discovered how much of ordinary life depended on magic. Elevators froze. Hospitals switched to emergency generators. Starships drifted in orbit. Streetlights vanished.
Then every speaker in the city whispered the same sentence: "You have been feeding me for six hundred years."
The reactors restarted.
The government called it a systems failure.

Chapter XXVII — The Ninth Bell
Years later, the ninth bell rang.
It was not a physical bell. Every clock in Stelio's struck nine at exactly the same instant, regardless of time zone, mechanical condition or power source.
Citizens remembered things they had forgotten: missing relatives, erased wars, secret laboratories, conversations they had never told anyone about.
Liora remembered the Factory.
So did millions of others.

Chapter XXVIII — The War of All Races
When the truth became public, no single government could control the reaction.
Dragons demanded reparations. Kitsune demanded the release of the Forgotten. Mayu engineers demanded access to Factory records. Rexouium pilots carried refugees between worlds. Ratfolk mechanics disabled military reactors without killing their operators. The Nyth opened their borders for the first time.
Armies formed, but the war never became one clean battlefield. It was a thousand uprisings, negotiations, rescues and betrayals across dozens of worlds.
For the first time in history, every major race had a reason to fight the same enemy.

Chapter XXIX — The Last Factory
The final Factory stood beneath Stelio's.
Inside were machines from every age of Aether civilization. Crystal engines. War cores. Infusion chambers. Dimensional frames. Prototype starship drives. Archives containing the names of people who had disappeared.
The machines did not attack the soldiers who entered.
They simply turned themselves off.
At the center of the complex, the oldest reactor opened like an eye.

Chapter XXX — The Frontier Beyond the Stars
No historian agrees on what happened after the reactor opened.
Some say the Void spoke. Others say it was the collective memory of every person ever used by the Factory. Some believe the Aether itself had become conscious long before anyone noticed.
What is known is this: the Frontier did not end.
The machines were rebuilt under new laws. The Forgotten received names again. The races created a shared council. Old enemies became neighbors. Children were taught the wars without being taught to hate the people who fought them.
And beyond the edge of the mapped galaxy, explorers found another star.
Its light was violet.
On its largest planet stood a door made of black glass.
Above the door were the same words first heard by Orun the dragon:
**YOU WERE NEVER THE FIRST.**

Stelio's began as a fortified research settlement built around the largest known Aether anomaly. It became a city, then an industrial capital, then one of the most influential political powers in the known Frontier.

The elevated districts are home to political leaders, wealthy industrial families, corporate executives and diplomatic missions. Private transit lanes and personal security separate this world from the streets below.

The commercial and governmental heart of Stelio's. Ministries, universities, arcane laboratories, entertainment districts and interstellar trade houses operate here beneath endless neon and holographic light.

Factories, workers, mechanics, musicians, underground clubs and communities built around the industrial economy. This is where the magic-punk identity of Stelio's is strongest—and where resentment toward the upper districts runs deepest.

Officially, the Deep does not exist. Beneath abandoned tunnels and forgotten infrastructure are sealed laboratories, criminal markets, pre-Stelio's ruins and routes that lead dangerously close to the foundations of the Magic Generation Factory.

## The Magic Generation Factory

**SECTION BRIEF:** How the Magic Generation Factory works, how citizens receive magic, the costs involved, and its most restricted secrets.

The official name is the **Stelio's Aetheric Energy Complex**. To ordinary citizens, it is simply the Factory: the industrial heart that provides magical power to transportation systems, defensive infrastructure, hospitals, starship docks, cybernetics and millions of Aether-infused citizens.

### How Citizens Gain Magic

Most modern citizens with access to legal facilities may receive an **Aether Infusion**. Refined Aether is introduced through a regulated procedure and bonds to the body's neurological and biological systems. The result differs dramatically between individuals. Biology, species, existing magical heritage and psychological factors influence what abilities emerge. Aether can amplify a Dragon's natural affinity, interact with a Kitsune's spiritual abilities or manifest in an entirely unexpected form within someone who previously possessed no magic at all.

### The Cost of Manufactured Magic

For centuries, Stelio's claimed that the Factory relied primarily on underground Aether extraction. Classified research eventually revealed a disturbing discovery: Aether becomes dramatically more active when exposed to living consciousness. Thought, memory and emotion can act as catalysts.

### RESTRICTED: THE SOUL FURNACES
During the earliest industrial experiments, prisoners and captured soldiers were used to test consciousness-based Aether extraction. The program expanded in secrecy. Political prisoners disappeared. Refugees were relocated into facilities that did not officially exist. Their identities were removed from public records.
THESE INDIVIDUALS BECAME KNOWN AS: THE FORGOTTEN.
Whether the Soul Furnaces still operate is one of the greatest political secrets in modern Stelio's.

## Politics & Power

**SECTION BRIEF:** The governments, factions, ideologies, power structures, and international politics that influence the Frontier.

Stelio's presents itself as a representative government. In reality, political influence is shared—and constantly contested—between the Aetheric Council, military command, industrial corporations, magical institutions and the populations that depend on all of them.

### The Aetheric Council

The Council contains twelve major seats representing military affairs, industry, arcane research, science, commerce, civilian interests, colonies, spiritual organizations, security, frontier interests, veterans and the Sovereign authority represented by the High Chancellor. Each seat has a constituency. Each also has allies, donors, enemies and access to information the others would prefer to control.

### Centralists
Believe Aether must remain regulated. Their argument is that unrestricted magical power would turn every political conflict into a catastrophe.
### Liberationists
Believe Aether is a universal resource and that Stelio's has no moral right to control access to magic.
### Industrialists
Support corporate development, patents and private ownership of magical technology.
### Traditionalists
Believe industrial civilization has corrupted ancient relationships with magic and pushed the world toward another disaster.
### Voidists
Range from philosophical researchers to dangerous cult movements. Some believe the Void is not an enemy but the next stage of civilization.

### International Politics

The Free Frontier rejects centralized authority and considers many Stelio's regulations a form of imperial control. The Corporate Dominion cooperates with the city while simultaneously attempting to gain independence from it. The Arcane Orders protect ancient magical knowledge but distrust industrial research. The Star Raiders operate across borders where governments have little practical power. These relationships are not static; trade agreements, assassinations, embargoes and military incidents can change alliances overnight.

## The Aether Wars

**SECTION BRIEF:** The Aether Wars and major conflicts that changed the political and technological landscape.

### The First Aether War

When neighboring powers learned that Stelio's had developed Aether Conversion technology, demands for access quickly became military threats. The Coalition of Nine Worlds formed against the growing city. The seventeen-year war saw dragons facing orbital fleets, magical sabotage campaigns and entire regions transformed into battlefields. The war ended after the deployment of a catastrophic Aether weapon remembered as **Crownfall**. Stelio's survived, but its victory convinced the government that control of Aether was a matter of national survival.

### The Second Aether War

The Free Aether Alliance formed around worlds that rejected Stelio's monopoly on magical resources. The resulting eleven-year conflict was not cleanly divided between heroes and villains. Rebel attacks destroyed civilian infrastructure while government forces conducted mass arrests and forced relocations. The Treaty of Black Glass ended open warfare but created political borders that remain disputed.

### The Third Aether Crisis

The most recent conflict began with assassinations and accusations between rival political movements. Then the Factory shut down for thirteen minutes. Across Stelio's, Aether-based technology failed. Millions of infused citizens reported hearing the same voice.

> “You have been feeding me for six hundred years.”

The government classified the event. The public never forgot it.

## Titles, Roles & Jobs

### High Chancellor
The political head of Stelio's. Negotiates treaties, approves emergency powers and coordinates government agencies. The Chancellor's greatest power is not command of every institution, but access to the information required to influence them.
### Grand Commander
The highest practical military authority beneath civilian leadership. Responsible for converting political objectives into campaigns, deployments and strategic operations.
### Fleet Admiral
Commands multiple naval formations and determines how military starships are deployed across contested systems.
### Captain
Commands an individual vessel. A Captain is responsible for crew safety, mission decisions, diplomacy and combat actions. On an independent ship, the Captain may also be owner, criminal, explorer or employer.
### Archmage
A recognized master of magical knowledge rather than automatically the strongest combatant. Archmages may lead research, preserve dangerous knowledge, advise governments or command arcane units.
### Arcane Engineer
Builds technology that depends on both engineering and magical theory: reactors, shields, weapons, cybernetics and portal systems.
### Aether Medic
Treats biological injury alongside magical trauma, Aether poisoning and damage caused by unstable infusions.
### Intelligence Agent
Collects information through investigation, surveillance, diplomacy and infiltration. Their success often prevents wars that the public never learns were possible.
### Frontier
An unofficial but respected identity for explorers, independent captains, surveyors and people who operate beyond stable civilization. A Frontier may work for a government—or answer to nobody.

## Roleplay Rules & Living Lore

**SECTION BRIEF:** The active roleplay rules and living-lore principles that govern character actions, consequences, consent, and canon.

The Arcane Frontier is designed to be played, not merely read. These rules establish how characters interact with the world while keeping the lore flexible enough for stories, factions, discoveries and player-driven consequences.

### 01 — Stay In Character
Characters should make decisions based on their knowledge, personality, abilities and circumstances. Players may know secrets their characters do not.
### 02 — No Forced Actions
Do not decide another player's thoughts, dialogue, injuries, death, relationships or actions without consent. Give other characters room to respond.
### 03 — Lore Has Weight
Established history, factions, ranks, magic limitations and major events matter. New ideas are welcome when they fit the setting or are introduced as discoveries, mutations, experiments or alternate traditions.
### 04 — Power Has Consequences
High-tier magic and machines are powerful, but they require training, resources, risk or authority. Rare abilities should create story rather than automatically solve every problem.
### 05 — Consent & Boundaries
Players control their own characters. Discuss major permanent changes, romantic developments, extreme violence, character death or irreversible consequences before enforcing them on another player.
### 06 — Earn Your Rank
Titles and ranks are part of the story. Characters gain recognition through training, achievements, service, discoveries, political influence or faction approval rather than simply declaring themselves the highest rank.
### 07 — Meta Knowledge

### 08 — Consequences Create Stories
Failure is not the end of a story. Injuries, damaged equipment, political suspicion, debt, reputation loss and magical instability can become opportunities for new roleplay.

### Roleplay Event Authority

Major events may be recognized by the Archive as **Canon Events**. Minor scenes can remain personal or local. Faction leaders, lore keepers and approved storytellers may introduce events that affect regions, organizations or the wider Frontier, provided they follow the setting's established rules.

### Personal Canon

### Faction Canon
Events affecting guilds, corporations, armies, magical orders or other organized groups.
### World Canon
Major discoveries, wars, disasters, political changes and lore revelations that affect the wider Frontier.

## Professions, Jobs & Class Careers — Role Dossiers

**SECTION BRIEF:** Roleplay careers and class/job structures, including responsibilities, progression, hybrid roles, and position templates.

In the Arcane Frontier, a **class** describes how a character tends to solve problems, a **job** describes what they are employed or trained to do, and a **rank** describes recognized authority, experience or institutional standing. They are deliberately separate. This allows a Tier II mage to be a respected medic, a Tier VI mage to be a junior researcher, or a non-magical mechanic to hold a command position because of experience.

These roles are not prison cells. A character can learn outside their profession, change careers, combine classes or become an unusual hybrid. The role exists to establish expectations, responsibilities, contacts, equipment and social consequences. In other words, the job tells the RP group *why this character matters when a problem appears*.

### How Roleplay Careers Work

### Class = Method
A class is the character's preferred approach: magic, engineering, combat, exploration, diplomacy or criminal expertise. It does not automatically grant a rank, salary or legal authority.
### Job = Responsibility
A job is the character's practical duty. It determines what other people reasonably expect them to know, maintain, investigate, protect or deliver.
### Rank = Recognition
Rank is earned through experience, certification, achievement, appointment or faction authority. A higher rank means greater responsibility or access, not automatically greater magical power.
### Reputation = Consequence
Reputation is what other characters believe about the person. It can rise or fall independently from formal rank and can open or close opportunities.

In roleplay, an Arcanist is often the person called when a magical problem does not fit a simple category. ### Career Progression Rule

Promotion should create new responsibilities instead of simply increasing a character's power. A junior Engineer might repair one reactor; a senior Engineer may be responsible for an entire district; a Chief Engineer may be legally accountable for the safety of a city or ship. Likewise, a junior Diplomat may attend meetings while an Ambassador can commit a government to a treaty. The higher the position, the larger the consequences of failure.

Characters should normally advance through demonstrated competence, completed assignments, training, faction recognition, mentorship or major story events. Exceptional characters can rise quickly, but rapid promotion should create its own problems: enemies may challenge their legitimacy, superiors may distrust their lack of experience, or the character may suddenly inherit responsibilities they are not ready to handle.

### Hybrid Roles

Hybrid characters are encouraged. A **Technomancer-Pilot** might maintain the Aether drive while flying a ship. A **Spellblade-Medic** might serve as a combat rescue specialist. A **Journalist-Hacker** could investigate corporate secrets. A **Void Scholar-Explorer** could lead expeditions into anomalous space. Hybridization should explain how the skills interact rather than granting every ability at full strength.

### Why the System Exists

The Frontier's professions are part of its history. The industrialization of Aether created engineers and artificers; the wars created marines, operatives and security specialists; interstellar expansion created pilots, Voidfarers, couriers and salvagers; megacities created fixers, hackers and corporate executives. Jobs therefore reveal what a society values, fears and needs to survive.

### Magic Tier Deep Classification

### Tier 0 — Dormant
Aether can be detected but cannot be reliably shaped. Characters may still be talented fighters, engineers or leaders.
### Tier I — Spark
Simple manifestations, minor wards, weak enhancements and basic utility.
### Tier II — Adept
Reliable combat and professional-grade magic. A character can sustain multiple practical techniques.
### Tier III — Expert
Complex combinations, specialized schools and effects capable of deciding small engagements.
### Tier IV — Master
Major battlefield, industrial or environmental influence. Masters can create techniques that become schools of study.
### Tier V — Grandmaster
Rare power capable of reshaping major conflicts, cities, strategic infrastructure or magical systems.
### Tier VI — Mythic
Extremely rare figures whose abilities become historical events. Tier VI magic is treated as strategic-level power.
### Tier Ω — Anomalous
Power that does not fit established theory. Ω cases are investigated because they may represent new physics, Void influence, artificial ascension or an unknown Aether phenomenon.

## The Frontier Beyond Stelio's

**SECTION BRIEF:** The Frontier beyond Stelio's: worlds, cities, space travel, and the major locations connected to the setting.

The Arcane Frontier is an interplanetary and interstellar setting. Worlds have their own economies, climates, histories and political identities. Travel is expensive and dangerous enough that distance still matters.

### The Core Worlds

### Stelio's
The industrial capital of the Arcane Frontier. Neon towers surround ancient foundations; corporate laboratories sit above worker districts; orbital elevators connect the city to starships. The city is the setting's largest meeting point for magic, cybernetics, organized crime and politics.
### Aurelia Prime
A wealthy metropolitan world built around colossal orbital rings. Its districts are famous for luxury arcologies, diplomatic towers, financial houses and artificial climates. The rich live above the weather; the working population maintains the machinery that makes that possible.
### Vanta IX
A perpetual-night mining world whose cities are carved into cliffs and underground caverns. Aether deposits make the planet strategically valuable. Smugglers and prospectors frequently disappear in the unregulated extraction zones.
### Helion Reach
A frontier system of shipyards, military stations and independent settlements. Its population contains veterans, mercenaries, refugees and explorers. Law exists, but distance makes enforcement inconsistent.
### Glassfall
A ruined world where an ancient Aether disaster vitrified huge regions of its surface. The glass plains contain dormant machines and magical structures that still activate without warning.
### The Null Belt
A region of space where conventional Aether readings become unreliable. Navigation systems frequently disagree, magical abilities behave unpredictably and abandoned stations transmit signals despite having no known power source.

### Major Cities

### Neon Bastion
A dense cyberpunk megacity where corporate security, street gangs, arcane clinics and underground markets coexist. Its famous Night Grid is an interconnected network of clubs, data markets and illegal machine workshops.
### Port Meridian
A neutral starport where governments, pirates, merchants and explorers can theoretically trade. Its oldest rule is simple: settle your disputes before they reach the docks.
### Blackglass City
Built around the site of a historic treaty, Blackglass is a diplomatic city filled with embassies, archives and intelligence services. It is one of the safest places to start a political storyline—and one of the most dangerous places to reveal a secret.
### Emberdeep
An industrial subterranean metropolis built around geothermal vents and Aether machinery. Mechanics, artificers and machine clans dominate its culture.

### Space Travel

Ships use combinations of conventional propulsion, Aetheric drives, jump gates and navigation beacons. Faster-than-light travel is possible, but it is not treated as casual teleportation: fuel, route permissions, navigation data, maintenance and anomalies determine how safely a crew can cross the Frontier.
